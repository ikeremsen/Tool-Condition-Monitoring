Part A · Step 1: What is actually in the NASA milling dataset?¶

Project question: can we estimate how worn a milling insert is (flank wear, VB) from signals measured while it cuts: acoustic emission, vibration and spindle motor current?

This first notebook trains no model. It checks what the data file really contains and compares it with the dataset README, because every later result is only as trustworthy as the data under it.

The experiment (from the README). A Matsuura MC-510V machining centre face-mills a 483 × 178 × 51 mm block with a 70 mm cutter carrying 6 Kennametal KC710 inserts, at a cutting speed of 200 m/min (826 rev/min). Eight conditions are tested: 2 depths of cut (0.75 / 1.5 mm) × 2 feeds (0.25 / 0.5 mm/rev) × 2 materials (cast iron / steel). Each condition is run twice with a fresh set of inserts, giving 16 cases. A run is one pass across the workpiece. Every few runs the insert was taken out and its flank wear VB was measured under a microscope.

What this shows

  • The 13 fields and 167 records match the README.
  • Signals are 9000 samples long, except in one record that has 15360 samples (section 5).
  • VB is stored as a float in 158 records and as an integer in 9. The 9 integers are all VB = 0 at run 1, which MATLAB happened to save as an integer. A run without a VB measurement is stored as NaN.

3. The label: flank wear VB¶

Flank wear (VB) is the width of the worn band on the insert's flank face, the face that rubs against the freshly cut surface. As VB grows, friction, cutting force and temperature rise, and surface finish gets worse. ISO 3685 commonly uses an average VB = 0.3 mm as the end-of-life criterion for carbide tools. It is drawn as a dashed reference line below, but the worn / not-worn threshold is a decision for Step 2.

The README warns that VB was not measured after every run. How many runs per case have a label?

What this shows

  • Missing labels: 21 of 167 runs have no VB (146 are labelled). Case 16 has only 3 of 6 labelled. In cases 12–16, run 1 is never labelled.
  • The curves have the classic three stages. There is a fast "running-in" rise, then a slow, nearly linear middle stretch, then accelerating wear near the end. The long cast-iron case 11 shows this most clearly.
  • Tool life scatters even under identical conditions. Compare the two cases inside one panel. For steel at 0.75 mm / 0.5 mm/rev, case 8 passes 0.3 mm after about 6 min and case 14 only after about 11 min. Cases 1 and 9 track each other for 30 min and then split. The README points out the same thing (material inhomogeneity, inclusions, coolant). Generalising from one insert to another is the real challenge here.
  • VB = 0 at run 1 in 9 cases. VB is supposed to be measured after the run, and an insert that has done a full pass has some wear. These zeros probably mean "new insert" rather than a real measurement.
  • VB decreases twice in case 1 (0.29 → 0.28 and 0.50 → 0.44). The insert was re-mounted and measured by eye under a microscope, so ±0.05 mm is a fair estimate of the label noise. A model error below that would be meaningless.
  • Several cases run far past the 0.3 mm criterion: case 13 reaches 1.53 mm.

4. The sensor signals¶

According to the README, the sensors are connected as follows:

Channel Sensor Processing before the computer
AE_table, AE_spindle acoustic emission (stress waves from plastic deformation and friction in the cutting zone) preamp with 50 kHz high-pass → amplifier → RMS meter, 8 ms
vib_table, vib_spindle accelerometers charge amp → filter → RMS meter, 8 ms
smcDC spindle motor current, DC portion none
smcAC spindle motor current, AC none

So four of the six channels are already RMS envelopes: a smoothed measure of signal energy, not the raw vibration or acoustic waveform. We can still look at levels and trends, but a frequency analysis of these channels cannot recover the original high-frequency content.

Engineering expectation: as the flank wears, friction between flank and workpiece rises, so cutting force rises. Spindle current is roughly proportional to torque and therefore to cutting force, so the current should go up. Acoustic emission from rubbing at the flank should go up too. Below, the same case is shown with a fairly sharp tool and with a worn one.

What this shows

  • Each 9000-sample record covers a whole pass: idle spindle, a step when the cutter enters (around sample 800–1000), steady cutting, and a decay as it leaves (around sample 7300–7700). The exact window shifts from run to run. Features should come from the in-cut window only. Otherwise the idle and transition parts dilute them.

  • With the worn tool:

    • the AC current amplitude grows (about ±3.5 V to about ±5.5 V);
    • AE_table and AE_spindle both rise;
    • smcDC hits a flat ceiling at about 10 V (section 5).

    This is the expected physics.

  • Both vibration channels are lower in the worn run (vib_table roughly halves). That is the opposite of the simple expectation. One run pair proves nothing, so later steps will check it across all runs. Possible causes include a real change in the cutting dynamics, or a change in sensor mounting or gain between runs.

5b. Clipping of the DC spindle current¶

smcDC never goes above 9.995 V, the top of the ±10 V input range. When the real current is higher, the recorded value sits flat at that ceiling: the signal is clipped.

Why this matters. Clipping is worst in the heavy-load cases with 1.5 mm depth of cut and 0.5 mm/rev feed (cases 1, 5, 9 and 16: on average 36–65% of samples clipped). It also appears in other cases once the tool is worn (for example steel cases 13–15), and it gets worse with wear. That makes physical sense: more wear means more force, which means more current. But it causes two problems:

  1. Features from smcDC saturate. The mean current stops rising at exactly the point where wear is high and most important to detect.
  2. The clipping itself is a shortcut. A model could learn "flat-topped current means worn", which partly means "heavy cutting condition" rather than "worn tool". On another machine with a different input range, that rule would fail.

smcAC is not clipped (it peaks around ±7 V), so it is the more reliable current channel. How to handle smcDC is a decision for the feature step.

6. Does the 250 Hz sampling rate add up?¶

The README says the RMS signals were sampled at 250 Hz. Three checks:

  1. Record length vs pass time. At 250 Hz, 9000 samples last 36 s. The cutter must travel about the workpiece length plus its own diameter, 483 + 70 ≈ 553 mm, to cross the block.
  2. Cut length vs feed. If the rate is fixed and each record covers the whole pass, halving the feed should double the number of in-cut samples.
  3. Spindle frequency. The AC current oscillates with the spindle rotation (826 rev/min = 13.8 Hz), which is the same in every case. Its peak frequency in cycles per sample tells us whether the sampling rate changed between runs.
In [13]:
pass_mm = nm.WORKPIECE_LENGTH_MM + nm.CUTTER_DIAMETER_MM
rows = []
for feed, g in clean.groupby("feed"):
    feed_mm_min = feed * nm.SPINDLE_RPM
    windows = [nm.in_cut_window(signals[i]["smcDC"]) for i in g["record"]]
    peaks = []
    for i in g["record"]:
        x = signals[i]["smcAC"] - signals[i]["smcAC"].mean()
        spectrum = np.abs(np.fft.rfft(x))
        freqs = np.fft.rfftfreq(x.size)          # cycles per sample
        peaks.append(freqs[5:][np.argmax(spectrum[5:])])   # skip the near-DC bins
    rows.append({
        "feed [mm/rev]": feed,
        "feed rate [mm/min]": feed_mm_min,
        "expected pass time [s]": 60 * pass_mm / feed_mm_min,
        "record length at 250 Hz [s]": 9000 / nm.FS_README_HZ,
        "median in-cut samples": np.median([e - s for s, e in windows]),
        "smcAC peak [cycles/sample]": np.median(peaks),
        "smcAC peak at 250 Hz [Hz]": np.median(peaks) * nm.FS_README_HZ,
    })
pd.DataFrame(rows).set_index("feed [mm/rev]").T.round(3)

What this shows

  • The rate looks constant between runs: the AC-current peak is almost the same in cycles per sample for both feeds.
  • At 250 Hz that peak would be about 12.3–12.9 Hz, close to the 13.8 Hz spindle frequency. So 250 Hz is plausible.
  • But the timing doesn't fit. A pass should take about 80 s (high feed) or about 160 s (low feed), yet a 36 s record shows the whole pass, including entry and exit. Halving the feed lengthens the in-cut section by only about 13% instead of 100%.

We can't resolve this from the file alone. The practical consequence: don't rely on the absolute time scale. Prefer features that don't depend on the exact sampling rate, such as statistics over the in-cut window, and treat any frequency in Hz with caution.
